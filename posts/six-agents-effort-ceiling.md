# Six parallel agents, roughly 140 search queries, and a ceiling that was not about effort

*2026-07-28 · ai-agents, growth, product-management, orchestration*

Scaling a partner program comes down to a growth-strategy question that shows up long before any spreadsheet does: do you push the same sourcing motion harder, or do you switch to a different one entirely. I run a partner and affiliate sourcing effort for a SaaS product, and for a while my working assumption was simple: if a search-based sourcing pass returns too few candidates, the fix is more search. More angles, more agents, more queries. Effort is the lever. That assumption held right up until a six-agent wave proved it wrong, and it proved it wrong in a way that took actual cross-checking to see, not just a gut feeling that something was off.

The setup was straightforward. An earlier, smaller sourcing round using two parallel agents had returned only a handful of new prospects, well under what I needed. My first reaction was to reject that yield outright and push for a much larger number. Before building the next batch, a colleague brought in for a product-thinking pass looked at existing visitor-analytics data and reframed the whole problem. The prior search had been built around a narrow question, who has reviewed this product by name, which topped out at a small handful of names. The better frame was different: who are the content creators who already hold the trust of an audience this product is trying to reach. A travel-blogging resource site, an agency-tools roundup blog, a food-blogger income-report writer. That reframe is what justified going wider, not just harder.

So we dispatched six parallel agents, each assigned its own search angle: agencies, travel bloggers, food bloggers, publishers and freelancers, a deliberately broadened general search net, and a new format angle, video creators on YouTube. Each agent had the same exclude list of existing prospects and the same instruction, non-negotiable this time, to check every email it found against the master list of known affiliates before returning anything. That instruction existed specifically because the earlier round had skipped that check and it later turned out one of its findings was already an active partner.

Here is where the assumption started to crack, and where the catch actually happened. Five of the six agents came back with genuinely new candidates, and across all six there were zero duplicate domains between any of the independent searches. On its face, that looked like a clean validation of the wider approach: six angles, mostly non-overlapping harvests, more coverage. But the more important signal was not the lack of overlap. It was what the agents reported about their own process. Every one of them ran its full query budget and reported genuine exhaustion or saturation, not early stopping, correctly disqualifying competitor self-promotion, content-farm sites, and coupon aggregators rather than padding them into the results just to hit a number. That is a very different failure mode than an agent giving up early. An agent giving up early means try again with more effort. Six agents running their entire budgets and still hitting a wall means the wall is not about effort.

The clearest single data point came from the video vertical. That agent returned zero new prospects, and it was a confirmed zero, not a stalled search. Every candidate channel it surfaced traced back to a creator who was already an active affiliate, or in one case to the program's own founder. There was no undiscovered pool left to search into. The other five verticals were not like this. Agencies returned one new domain, travel bloggers returned the largest batch by far, food bloggers, publishers and freelancers, and the broadened general search each returned a smaller handful, twenty three new prospects combined across the whole wave. So the honest reading was not that the wave failed. It was that five of six angles still had room, and one had none left at all, and the six-agent effort had cost roughly one hundred forty distinct search queries combined to establish that.

The actual mechanism, once I sat with it, was obvious in retrospect. Query-by-query search against a content vertical is bounded by how many individually-authored sites or channels exist in that vertical, not by how many queries you throw at it or how many agents you run in parallel. A vertical like YouTube affiliate marketers in this space is a small, mostly-known population. Searching it harder does not create new creators. It just re-finds the same ones faster. Running six agents instead of two multiplies coverage across angles, but within any single angle it does not change the size of the underlying population you are drawing from. That is the distinction I had missed. I had been treating "not enough results" as an execution problem when it was actually a population-size problem, and the tell was that agents were reporting real, correct exhaustion rather than sloppiness.

The fix that became standing growth-strategy practice going forward was to stop treating a low-yield sourcing wave as a signal to run more agents of the same shape, and instead to check first whether the agents involved reported genuine query exhaustion with disciplined disqualification of bad candidates. If they did, that is the confirmation to switch sourcing mechanisms entirely, moving from query-by-query search toward a bulk-data approach that starts from a much larger existing dataset and filters down, rather than searching outward one query at a time. Query-by-query search is a fine way to find the last handful of candidates in a saturated vertical. It is a poor way to reach a target that requires an order of magnitude more than any single angle can hold, and mistaking one for the other is how a growth team ends up spending another quarter on a channel that was already tapped out.

The generalizable lesson is that "we need more effort" and "we need a different mechanism" produce the same symptom, a low result count, and the only way to tell them apart is to check whether the effort that was actually spent was spent well before deciding what to fund next. A tool or an agent stopping early looks identical from the outside to a tool or an agent hitting a real, structural ceiling, unless you specifically verify how it spent its budget and whether what it discarded was correctly discarded. Skip that check and you will keep adding effort, and budget, to a wall that effort was never going to move.
