# The metric did not improve. The denominator changed.

*2026-07-28 · analytics, product, data-integrity, metrics*

A conversion rate is rarely just a number. It is usually the evidence a team points to when deciding where to keep investing. For a long stretch, I had a number I quoted with real confidence for exactly that purpose: Full CVR, running around two percent for Organic and Direct traffic on a version of the website we called V7, cited as proof that a redesign had paid off and that those channels deserved continued attention. It looked decent. It sat next to other numbers, in other reports, that told an uglier story: the same metric, same name, sitting under one percent for the same channels. I treated the gap as a scheduling problem. Different reports pulled data on different days, over different windows, so naturally they would not line up perfectly. I was wrong about what kind of problem it was, and the mistake mattered because a real decision about where to keep investing was riding on which of the two numbers was true.

The trigger for catching it was arithmetic, not suspicion. One report blended Organic and Direct traffic into a single conversion figure that dropped from around 0.37 percent to 0.34 percent over a thirty-five day window. Another report, covering roughly seventeen days inside that same period, had Organic at 2.18 percent and Direct at 2.60 percent. Both numbers were labeled "Full CVR." A blend of two channels each running above two percent cannot mathematically average down to a third of a percent. Something in the two reports was not measuring the same thing, and the gap was too large to be sampling noise.

My first working theory was window length. The thirty-five day blended window, I reasoned, was probably averaging together a short high-conversion stretch with a longer low-conversion stretch, and that averaging was what was dragging the number down. It is an intuitive theory, and it is the kind of theory that feels complete the moment you say it out loud, which is exactly why I did not stop there.

I ran the actual pulls instead of trusting the intuition. Fourteen days ending on the blended report's own end date: Direct came back at 0.41 percent, Organic Search at 0.50 percent. Fourteen days ending on the most recent date available: Direct 0.23 percent, Organic Search 0.43 percent. Both were consistent with the thirty-five day blend, not with the 2.18/2.60 figure. Then I tried to reconstruct the specific roughly seventeen-day window the original V7 report claimed to cover, running it two slightly different ways to account for uncertainty about its exact boundaries: Direct came back between 0.58 and 0.63 percent, Organic Search between 0.97 and 0.98 percent. Still nowhere near two percent.

Four separate windows, four different lengths, and Organic and Direct conversion stayed under one percent sitewide in every single one of them. That was the finding that ruled out window length as the explanation: if a short high-conversion sub-period had been dragging a longer average down, changing the window length should have moved the Organic and Direct numbers around. It did not, across four separate attempts at changing it. In the one window where I also pulled Paid Search and Referral for comparison, both ran well above two percent, which told me the low sitewide numbers were not some general depression across every channel, just those two. Something else was different about how the 2.18/2.60 figure had been produced in the first place, and it was not time.

I went back to the script that had generated the original number, recovered from an older session log rather than from the missing report itself. The V7 figure had been computed as purchases per day divided by home-page sessions per day, per channel: a denominator restricted to sessions that had actually entered the funnel by touching the home page. I checked this formula against a previously published figure to make sure I had the right method, not a guess: 0.43 purchases per day divided by 14.7 home-page sessions per day comes out to 2.93 percent, which matches the 2.91 percent that had been published for that version. The formula checked out.

The other report's pull used a completely different scope: a channel dimension that GA4 assigns to every session sitewide, whether that session ever touched the funnel or not. Same purchase counts, two denominators of very different sizes, same label, "Full CVR," on both. A funnel-scoped denominator is mechanically smaller than a sitewide one, so the identical numerator produces a much larger rate. That is the whole mechanism. Nobody had made an error in either individual pull. The error was treating two structurally different metrics as if they were one metric that had simply moved.

The fix was not a data cleanup. It was a naming decision. The two figures got separated into two distinctly labeled metrics, reported in separate tables rather than compared in the same row: a funnel-scoped figure for measuring whether a specific page-to-purchase path was improving, and a sitewide figure, treated as the one trusted metric for any claim about overall channel performance, transactions divided by sessions, per channel. Once they had different names, they stopped being comparable by accident, and every future report inherited that separation as a default rather than a one-time fix. That matters beyond the report itself: any future call about which channel deserves more investment now has to specify which of the two metrics it is standing on, instead of inheriting whichever number happened to be closest at hand.

The lesson I keep coming back to is that a metric name is a promise about what was measured, and nothing enforces that promise automatically. Two pulls can share a label, a dashboard, even a reporting cadence, and still be answering different questions, because the definition lives in a script or a query, not in the name printed above the number. For a product decision, that check has to happen before the number goes into a slide, not after. Any time a metric moves in a way that surprises you, the first question worth asking is not what changed in the world, but what changed in the denominator, and whether the thing on the other side of the comparison was ever measuring the same thing at all.
